## Bottom line up front

This entire literature has one dominant lesson, repeated three times across 35 years: **demographic housing models built on cohort/age structure alone have a near-perfect record of being wrong on price, and a mixed-to-poor record even on quantities (turnover/listings).** The single mechanism that keeps killing these forecasts is the same one every time — treating a partial-equilibrium demand identity as if it were a closed system, when supply elasticity, financing conditions (rates), income/wealth trajectories, and migration inflows are all endogenous responses that move in the opposite direction from what the simple cohort story implies. Any model you build in this class needs to be explicit about which of these four offsets it is assuming away, because the literature's autopsies are unanimous that ignoring them is exactly what broke the canonical forecasts.

## 1. Mankiw & Weil (1989) — the original sin, and why it failed

Mankiw and Weil, "The Baby Boom, the Baby Bust, and the Housing Market," *Regional Science and Urban Economics* 19 (1989): 235–258, ran single-variable cross-sectional regressions on 1970/1980 Census microdata regressing self-reported house value on a person's age (single-year dummies). They found housing demand rising sharply from age 20 to a peak around 36–40, then declining roughly 1%/year of age thereafter. Extrapolating that age profile onto the known population pyramid (the Baby Boom aging past 40, replaced by the smaller Baby Bust cohort), they forecast **real house prices would fall ~47% (≈3%/year) between 1987 and 2007**. It caused a sensation — media coverage, immediate scholarly pile-on.

**What actually happened:** real house prices *roughly doubled* 1987–2007 per Case-Shiller (Green & Lee, 2015, USC working paper "Age, Demographics, and the Demand for Housing, Revisited"). Even after the 2008 bust, real prices in 2014 were ~39% higher than in 1987 — the opposite sign of the forecast, by a wide margin.

**Autopsy literature's verdict on why it failed** (this is well-settled, not contested):
1. **Cohort effect vs. age effect — the single biggest miss** (Pitkin & Myers 1994; Green & Hendershott 1996; Myers 1999). Mankiw-Weil implicitly assumed the age-demand profile is *fixed across generations*. It isn't. Boomers were dramatically better-educated and higher-earning over their lifetimes than the generations Mankiw-Weil's 1970/1980 cross-sections actually measured aging past 40 — those earlier cohorts had *lower* lifetime income, so their declining consumption after 40 was a cohort artifact (poorer people, who happened to be older in that cross-section), not a true aging effect. Green & Hendershott (1996), using Rosen's (1974) two-stage hedonic model, decomposed a "partial derivative" (pure age, controlling for income/education) that shows demand *rising* even past 40, versus the "total derivative" that replicates Mankiw-Weil's inverted-U. The gap is almost entirely cohort composition.
2. **Cross-section regression used as a dynamic forecast** — a conceptual category error flagged by nearly every commentator (Woodward 1991, Hamilton 1991, Holland 1991, Hendershott 1991) and reiterated as "the most important conceptual problem" in later retrospectives.
3. **Non-stationary time series variables** misused in the forecasting step (Holland 1991, Hendershott 1991).
4. **Ignored supply response** — Woodward (1991) and Hamilton (1991) noted the model implicitly assumed housing supply couldn't respond to changing demand; in reality supply elasticity (or lack thereof, in supply-constrained coastal metros) is a first-order determinant of price, independent of demographics.
5. **Interest rates / user cost** — Hendershott (1991), after adding real after-tax interest rates and their rate of change as controls, found *much* more modest predicted declines than the raw Mankiw-Weil number — meaning a chunk of the "47%" was really an omitted-variable artifact of the rate environment, not demographics per se.
6. **Income growth** compounded with the cohort-education effect: boomers' higher permanent income meant sustained high housing consumption per household even as the generation aged, directly contradicting the "flat consumption-across-cohorts" assumption baked into the extrapolation.
7. Mankiw and Weil themselves (1991 reply) contended their critics hadn't reached consensus — but by the mid-1990s the profession had converged on the cohort-effect explanation as dispositive (Green & Lee 2015 call this the settled account).

Notably absent from that list, and worth flagging explicitly for your purposes: **none of the standard autopsies lean heavily on immigration** as the reason M-W failed — the fix is almost entirely a domestic cohort-composition/income/rate/supply story. Immigration becomes central only in the *later* literature (Myers, and the Fannie Mae work below), which is a genuinely separate strand.

## 2. Dowell Myers — the more careful, modern version, and its actual track record so far

Myers is a USC demographer, not an economist, and his approach is methodologically distinct from Mankiw-Weil in a way that matters: it uses **cohort homeownership retention rates**, not age-price regressions.

- **Myers & Ryu, "Aging Baby Boomers and the Generational Housing Bubble: Foresight and Mitigation of an Epic Transition,"** *Journal of the American Planning Association* 74(1), Winter 2008 (won JAPA's Best Article award). Core claim: sellers of existing homes supply ~85% of annual home sales, home sales are driven by the age structure of *sellers* not just buyers, seniors are net sellers, and the senior:working-age ratio would rise ~67% over two decades — creating conditions for a "generational housing bubble" if the smaller/less-advantaged following generations can't absorb the boomer sell-off. Framed explicitly as foresight-for-mitigation (planning-policy prescriptions), not a point forecast of price collapse — a more hedged framing than Mankiw-Weil from the start.

- **Myers & Simmons (Fannie Mae), "The Coming Exodus of Older Homeowners,"** Fannie Mae Housing Insights, ~2018 (I pulled and read the full primary source). This is the methodological core you should copy if you build this model:
  - **Method:** track the *cohort homeownership retention rate* — ratio of homeowners in a birth cohort at the end of a decade to the same cohort's count at the start — using Census/ACS data back to 1980. This sidesteps needing separate population projections.
  - **Key empirical finding, and it is the load-bearing one:** retention rates for cohorts aging 55-64→65-74 have been stable at 0.92–0.95 across four decades regardless of economic climate; but for 65-74→75-84 only ~67-75% of homeowners are retained per decade, and for 75+→85+ only ~26-31% remain after ten years. **The exodus is real and structural but it hits hard only in the late-70s/80s age bracket, not at 65.**
  - **Explicit refusal to over-claim on price:** the report is careful to separate an "age-group" statistical illusion (more 65-74-year-old homeowners exist over time simply because the large Boomer cohort is filling that bracket) from the "cohort" reality (the *same* people, tracked over time, lose ~1.1M owners per decade as that specific cohort ages). It projects **13.1-14.6 million older-homeowner exits 2026-2036, ≥42% more than the prior decade** — but stops short of a price forecast. It explicitly flags two mitigating channels it cannot resolve: (a) **not all boomer-vacated homes hit the market — some are bequeathed to heirs who retain ownership**; (b) **immigration**: foreign-born households accounted for **41% of the net increase in U.S. homeowners 2014-2016** (20 points of that from immigrants who arrived in 2010 or later) — i.e., the paper names immigrant homebuying as the plausible demand-side offset, years before this became a mainstream talking point, and explicitly says lower future immigration would remove that offset.
  - As of the 2018 writing, the report notes Boomer homeownership counts had *not yet* declined (2013-2016 ACS: total Boomer owner-occupant households fell <0.5%; even the oldest Boomer sub-cohort fell <3%) — i.e., even Myers' own more careful model was, at time of writing, still pre-exodus.

**Track record check, 2018→2026:** the exodus Myers projected to accelerate through 2026-2036 has, per every current data source I found (Redfin 2025-26 surveys, Zillow, HousingWire, JCHS Harvard 2023), **not materialized as inventory yet** — see item 5. Myers' framework is more defensible than Mankiw-Weil's because it never made a price call and built in the immigration/inheritance offset from the start, but the "exodus" itself is running behind even his own more conservative schedule, likely because rate lock-in (a channel neither Myers nor Mankiw-Weil modeled — both predate the 2022-2024 rate shock) is suppressing exits further.

## 3. Japan — the one realized case, and the actual lesson

Japan is as close as this literature gets to a live-fire test, because it has both the demographic pattern (peak population 2008, since declining; 30% of population over 65) and multiple decades of realized data. The result is **not** "aging causes nationwide price decline" — it's sharp bifurcation by geography:

- **Periphery/rural Japan:** ~9 million vacant homes nationally as of 2023 (13.8% of all residences, a record). Regional prefectures (Tohoku, rural Shikoku, parts of Chugoku) show flat-to-declining land prices as of the 2024/2025 MLIT land price survey. Mechanism: elderly owners die or move to care facilities, heirs (concentrated in the cities) don't want the rural home, renovation costs exceed value, and post-death property-tax reclassification can push annual tax on vacant land 6x higher than for occupied land — pushing owners toward "akiya banks," donation, or abandonment rather than a normal market sale.
- **Tokyo/core metro:** the opposite regime entirely. 2024 MLIT survey: Tokyo ward *residential* land +7.9% y/y, *commercial* +11.8%, fourth consecutive year of gains; nationwide standard land prices rose at the fastest pace in 34 years in the 2025 survey (highest since the 1992 bubble collapse). Major regional cities that function as regional hubs (Sapporo, Sendai, Hiroshima, Fukuoka) also rose, in some metrics *faster* than Tokyo (+5.8% y/y) — driven by internal migration concentrating young workers in a shrinking set of urban labor markets.

**The teachable lesson for your model:** aggregate national demographic decline does not translate into a uniform national price effect. It reallocates population toward a shrinking number of core metros (job/amenity draw), so the demographic story bites hardest at the **periphery of a shrinking country**, while core metros can show demographically-contrarian price gains driven by *internal* migration concentration, foreign investment, and land-supply constraints. If you're modeling Canada/Québec, this maps directly onto Montréal-core vs. off-island Québec — a national aggregate demographic model will misprice both tails.

## 4. Canada / Québec

- **CMHC housing-supply-gap framing (2023 "Solving the Affordability Crisis," updated Spring 2026 reports):** estimates a **2.6 million unit** supply gap needed by 2035 to restore affordability, with immigration-led population growth named as the dominant demand driver, and construction/approval-process constraints as the dominant supply-side bottleneck. Household formation hit a record **482,000 new households in 2024**, but CMHC's own baseline has this **falling sharply in 2025-2026 and staying below historical average through 2030**, explicitly because of the 2024 federal immigration-level cuts — i.e., CMHC's own model treats immigration policy, not boomer aging, as the swing factor in the medium term.
- **CMHC seniors-specific data (2023-24 reporting, using StatCan census cohorts):** this is your best Canadian empirical anchor and it points the *same direction* as the U.S. aging-in-place finding — **declining, not rising, senior sale rates**: of homeowners over age 75, **38.6% sold their property in the 1996-2001 window vs. only 36% in 2016-2021**. CMHC economist Francis Cortellino attributes this to better health/wealth extending independent living, and a lack of appropriately-sized/located alternative housing stock. StatCan's own aging projections put the 85+ cohort's growth surge at 2030-2040 — i.e., CMHC's own read is that Canada is not yet inside the demographic window where a senior sell-off would show up even if the model were otherwise correct.
- **Québec-specific wrinkles, all independently verified this session:**
  - **Natural decrease is now real, not projected:** Québec recorded 80,450 deaths vs. 78,200 births in 2025 (deaths > births for the second consecutive year; natural growth first went negative in 2024) — ISQ, per Desjardins Nov 2025 and ISQ's own "Births, deaths, migration" 2025 communiqué.
  - **Interprovincial net outflow widened to ~7,600 people in 2025**, primarily to Ontario and Alberta (ISQ).
  - **Immigration is provincially controlled and the federal 2024 pivot (reduced study permits, non-permanent-resident caps) is already showing up in Montréal**: falling vacancy near university neighborhoods as international students thin out; Desjardins (Nov 2025, Jul 2025, Mar 2026 housing spotlights) frames Québec's housing demand as **cooling because of the NPR/immigration drawdown**, not warming from demographics, with Desjardins explicitly warning of "prospect of population decline in 2025" and increased risk of a market correction if this compounds with a tariff-driven recession.
  - One long-range population projection cited (Mortgage Sandbox, using StatCan cohort projections) has **Montréal shrinking ~4.5% while Québec City grows ~21%** over three decades — another periphery-vs.-core-style bifurcation, this time *within* the same province and running in the opposite geographic direction from the US/Japan intuition (the traditional core, Montréal, is the one projected to lose population, not gain it) — worth stress-testing before you assume "Montréal = safe core metro" by analogy to Tokyo/NYC.
  - I found **no Québec-specific academic or bank study that explicitly nets out "boomer wealth release vs. immigrant-driven household formation"** the way Myers/Fannie Mae did for the U.S. — this appears to be a genuine gap; the closest analogues are the CMHC national seniors data (not Québec-specific) and the Desjardins immigration-driven demand spotlights (not boomer-specific). If you build this model for the Québec market, you'd be doing the first serious synthesis of these two threads for this geography, not replicating an existing study.

## 5. Did the US "senior sell-off" materialize post-2020? No — and the mechanism is well-identified now

Every current-vintage source (2024-2026) says the same thing: **the "silver tsunami" has not hit as inventory.**
- Redfin 2026 survey: **78% of older homeowners plan to stay in their homes as they age.**
- Earlier Clever Real Estate 2024 survey: **~54% of boomer owners say they never plan to sell**, citing "fits their lifestyle" (52%) or explicit "prefer to age in place" (47%); 40% cite a paid-off mortgage keeping housing costs low.
- **Mortgage rate lock-in is the mechanism that neither Mankiw-Weil nor Myers (writing pre-2022) could have modeled:** each percentage point that market rates exceed a homeowner's origination rate reduces sale probability by ~18.1%; this lock-in effect is estimated to have suppressed fixed-rate-mortgage home sales by ~57% in a recent quarter (over 1 million prevented sales) — Fortune/AEI reporting on academic lock-in estimates, 2024.
- **Freddie Mac (Feb 2024)** is the one bullish-supply voice, projecting 9 million boomer homes will reach market over the next decade — but even boosters concede (Fortune, Dec 2023) the field is "split," and by 2025-26 the consensus (Zillow, Redfin, HousingWire) had shifted to "trickle, not tsunami," explicitly citing:
  - the geographic mismatch — empty-nester boomer inventory concentrates in already-affordable markets, not the expensive coastal/urban labor markets that actually need supply;
  - a genuine downsizing-housing shortage — there's nowhere smaller/accessible to move *to*, so even willing sellers stay put;
  - and JCHS Harvard's "Housing America's Older Adults 2023" reframes the whole issue as a *care and cost-burden* crisis (record 11.2M cost-burdened older adults in 2021) rather than a supply-release event — the demographic wave is real, but it's manifesting as unmet care/accessibility need, not housing turnover.
- Bottom line for your model: **the U.S. senior-sell-off prediction failed on timing, same as Mankiw-Weil failed on direction** — the mechanism (aging in place + rate lock-in + no downsizing stock) is now well enough understood that a serious model has to include a lock-in / friction term, not just a demographic stock-flow identity.

## 6. Intergenerational transfer vs. market sale — the fraction that matters and is usually left out

This is the parameter most likely to make or break a demographic housing model, and it's underused in the literature you asked about:
- Myers/Fannie Mae (2018) flag it qualitatively ("not all homes vacated by Boomers will hit the sales market, as some will be bequeathed to heirs who opt to retain ownership") but **do not quantify** the split.
- The best quantitative estimate I found (Trust & Will / Talker Research survey, consumer-survey methodology, not government data — treat as directional, not authoritative): **of past inheritances, 73% became a transaction (sold or rented) vs. 27% retained purely as a personal residence; 56% of past heirs actually sold** (vs. only 35% of *future* heirs who currently *plan* to sell — i.e., people systematically underestimate how often financial/maintenance/family pressure forces a later sale). Only 17% of past heirs kept-and-rented, vs. 36% of future heirs who currently plan to.
- The load-bearing implication: **even most inherited homes eventually become market listings** — the "kept by heirs forever" scenario that softens the Myers/Fannie Mae exodus projection is a *delay* mechanism (heirs often keep for a period before selling under pressure), not a permanent absorption of the supply. If you're modeling flow-through of the boomer housing stock, a reasonable working assumption from this data is heir-retention buys a few years' delay on maybe a quarter to a third of exits, but doesn't remove that supply from the eventual market pool — meaning the "exodus" question is really a question about *timing and rate* of listing, not whether the listings ultimately arrive.
- I could not find rigorous academic (as opposed to survey-house) research directly quantifying the market-sale vs. heir-retention split at the population level for the U.S., and found **nothing Canada/Québec-specific** on this question at all — this is the thinnest-evidenced sub-question of the six, and worth flagging as a genuine research gap rather than something you can cite a hard number for.

## What this means for building the model

Every generation of this literature failed or stalled for a reason that's now identifiable and, more importantly, *repeatable* if you don't design around it:
1. Mankiw-Weil died on cohort-vs-age confound + ignored supply/rate/income endogeneity.
2. Myers/Fannie Mae is more careful (retention-rate cohort method, explicit immigration/inheritance offsets flagged) but its own "exodus" is running late because it couldn't anticipate the 2022-2024 rate-lock-in shock — a reminder that *any* structural demographic model needs a financing-conditions term bolted on, because financing regimes change faster than demographics and can dominate the sign of the near-term effect.
3. Japan shows the effect is real but geographically concentrated at the periphery, invisible or reversed at the core — a national/provincial aggregate will wash this out; you need geographic granularity (Montréal-core vs. off-island Québec vs. Québec City, at minimum) to get a signal instead of noise.
4. Canada/Québec-specific synthesis of "boomer release vs. immigrant absorption" does not yet exist as a rigorous study — building it would be original work, not replication, and the Québec case adds two features (provincially-controlled immigration levels that can be cut by policy fiat, and already-realized natural decrease) that make it a genuinely different — and arguably cleaner — natural experiment than the U.S. case, precisely because the immigration lever is more exogenous and more visible in the data than in the U.S.
